# SEED-01: The Extraction Paradox

**Theme:** Platform defaults as invisible governance; how extraction becomes normalized through architecture

**Bridge:** Architecture shapes behavior ↔ Capital cognitive capture ↔ Digital sovereignty

---

## Working Title

Why Default Decisions Feel Like Consent

## One-Sentence Thesis

Platform defaults work because they're *invisible governance*, not because users consciously choose them—making extraction feel inevitable rather than chosen.

## Reader Promise

Understand why "just accept the terms" fails as a model for digital autonomy, and what alternative architectures could look like.

---

## Novel Connections

1. **[[Default Decisions - Architecture Shapes Behaviour]] ↔ [[Capital Cognitive Capture]]** — Architecture silence is extraction permission. Defaults don't just shape behavior; they *harvest* behavior as training data. Most analyses treat these as separate problems (governance vs. datafication) when they're the same mechanism.

2. **[[Framework - Architecture-Behaviour-Outcomes Model]] ↔ [[Digital Sovereignty]]** — The framework names the mechanism; sovereignty names what's lost. The gap is agency erosion: you can't exercise sovereignty if the architecture doesn't *allow* non-default choices.

---

## Core Throughline

- Defaults appear neutral but encode extraction intent
- Consent requires *real choice* (not just acknowledging existence of settings)
- Alternative: architectures designed for sovereignty-first (opt-in, visible, changeable by default)

## Where It Needs Explanation

- How architecture *hides* the presence of choice (visibility problem)
- Why "transparency" alone doesn't solve extraction (people still don't change defaults)
- What "sovereignty-first design" looks like concretely (examples, not theory)
- The difference between "complicity by ignorance" vs. "complicity by design"

---

## Tension / Counterpoint

- **Counterpoint:** Users *like* defaults (reduce decision fatigue). So extract-first defaults aren't a design flaw; they're a feature.
- **Your position:** True, but that's precisely why they're extractive. The convenience is the trap.

## First Paragraph Starter

> You check "I agree" without reading the terms. Everyone does. We tell ourselves we're making a choice, but defaulting is not choosing—it's *accepting the chosen*. The real design question is never "what should the default be?" It's "who got to decide that this would be the default?" And that answer, almost always, is whoever profits from your compliance.

## Editor Margin Notes

- **Get specific:** LinkedIn's AI scraping default vs. browser auto-update default vs. cookie consent. Show the pattern isn't universal.
- **Avoid:** Abstract "big tech is bad." Focus on mechanism: defaults work because they're *cheaper than designing choice*.
- **Missing:** How individuals/small orgs can resist this (technical + cultural tactics).
- **Cut if fluff:** Don't re-explain why defaults are efficient. Reader knows. Focus on extraction mechanism.
